The previous chapter described the statistical properties of the CIC-IDS2018\supercite{cicids2018Official} dataset and established the analytical foundations required to interpret the noise-injection experiments. The present chapter describes the full pipeline through which those experiments were designed and executed: from the initial hyperparameter optimization on a small representative sample of the data, through the derivation of the feature set on which all subsequent experiments operate, to the training of baseline models and the systematic protocol used to evaluate the impact of PuckTrick-injected corruptions.

This section of the thesis will discuss various applications of the \texttt{Pucktrick} library, but our main goal for this thesis will be developing following experiment: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \red{Experiment A:} \textbf{Experiments performed using a reduced dataset}: These experiments serve as a proof of concept, providing evidence that the introduction of controlled noise into the dataset may positively influence the effectiveness of deep learning-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs). The main objective of this phase was to investigate whether specific noise injection techniques could systematically improve model performance; consequently this set of experiments was carried out on a preprocessed version of the dataset in which redundant features (i.e., highly correlated features) as well as zero-variance features had been removed. This was done in order to retain only features that actually affect learing of the selected models, while also decreasing the computational power required to evaluate all experiments.S Note that this is not main focus of the thesis, yet, just a way to prove one of the many applications of the newly developed Pucktrick functionality. For this reason those experiment are more limited, as they're experiment were conducted only on 5 random seeds. 

    \item \red{Experiment B:} \textbf{Experiments performed using the complete dataset}: unlike the previous scenario, this experimental setting relied on the original dataset without applying feature reduction procedures (such as removing redundant features and removing constant features). The purpose of this phase is primarily analytical, focusing on the theoretical aspects of the study. More specifically, it aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the behavior of the selected models when exposed to noise generated through the \texttt{Pucktrick} library. Those are the main focus of the thesis and have been developed using a wider range of 20 random seeds. Differently from previous experiment, in this case study after applying noise to each feature one by one there will also be a dedicated section to noise injection of multiple features simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:exp-protocol-features}
% =======================================================================

The present section provides a high-level summary of the experimental pipeline composed by: encompassing hyperparameter optimization, feature importance extraction, combined feature ranking, baseline training, and the systematic noise-injection experiment loop. 

\subsection{Feature Set for the Experiments}
\label{sec:feature-set-summary}

Following hyperparameter optimization on a 0.1\% stratified sample, feature importance scores were extracted (for both Experiments tasks) from both models (TabNet's native attention-based scores and CNN-LSTM's permutation importance). Those were then combined into a joint average rank.  A stratified selection procedure was then applied to this ranking to derive a compact set of \textbf{10 features} covering the \emph{full} spectrum of both:
\begin{itemize}
    \item feature types: covering full spectrum of continuous, categorical, binary, and date-time features
    \item fragility levels: covering full spectrum of high, medium, low fragility scores (for both label classes) previously derived during dataset analysis in Chapter \ref{sec:feat-target-corr}
\end{itemize}   

\subsection{Noise-Injection Experiment Grid}
\label{sec:exp-grid-summary}

PuckTrick was then applied:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For \red{Experiment A}: exclusively to \emph{one column} of the \emph{training partition} dataset; the validation and test sets remain clean throughout all experiments.  
    \item For \red{Experiment B}: was applied in the same way as the latter, plus it was also applied in a different branch of experiment to multiple features simultaneously. 
\end{itemize}

The experiment grid spans:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Five corruption methods}: \texttt{missing}, \texttt{noise}, \texttt{outliers}, \texttt{labels}, \texttt{duplicated}.
    \item \textbf{Six corruption intensities}: $p \in \{5\%,\,10\%,\,20\%,\,35\%,\,50\%,\,75\%\}$.
    \item \textbf{Five random seeds} for \red{Experiment A} and \textbf{Twenty random seeds} for \red{Experiment B}: ensuring statistical robustness of the observed performance deltas.
\end{itemize}

For each combination, two selected models (TabNet and CNN-LSTM) are retrained from scratch using the best hyperparameters identified during optimization, and the resulting evaluation metrics (MCC, F1, AUC, confusion matrices) are persisted to disk for subsequent analysis. Each of those experiments were then repeated for each feature of the dataset by applying noise injection \emph{one feature at a time}. And then for \red{Experiment B} were then repeated \red{fix number} more times while applying noise injection to multiple features at a time. 

Also more experiments were then practiced on each of the two labels (by injecting noise independently), adding to the grid: \textbf{One corruption methods} (Only \texttt{labels}, applied to both the target features, injecting noise one feature at a time), \textbf{Six corruption intensities} (same as before), \textbf{Twenty random seeds}: (The same ones used for the previuous experiment on non-target features).

This makes results shown in Chapter \ref{sec: Cumulative BreakEven Point Test} extremely relevant, as of already shown, applying all 5 \texttt{Pucktrick} noise injection methods one by one a single column \emph{with newly implemented spark backend} allows us to apply transformation 8 times faster than relying on the old Pandas backend: allowing us to save \emph{huge} amount of time.

\section{Models selection}
\label{Chap: model selection}
% =======================================================================

In order to develop this work, many different types of models were tested. Yet only the following two made the final cut, each for different reasons:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tabnet}: This state of the art model was chosen as it showed giving very promising results on F1-scores both during hyper-parameter tuning and baselines study (where no noise was introduced). Another very important characteristic that made us choose this model is it's ability to give a global feature importance ranking. This model was chosen as our golden standard for processing our CIC-IDS2018 dataset as tabular data.
    \item \textbf{CNN-LSTM}: This model was directly taken from the "CIC-IDS-using deep leaning"\supercite{jablaoui2024effective} paper. This work operated in a similar manner regarding what we wanted to do in this thesis by using the same dataset and developing same CNN-LSTM as proof of concept that adding some LSTM layers to a CNN (therefore allowing the model to process temporal data) could improve the learning performances. This model was used as our golden standard for processing the CIC-IDS2018 dataset as a time series, as already proved from this paper that this model was more than capable in succeeding this task. 
\end{itemize}

More detailed informations on both this models, as well as the one that didn't make the final cut will be discussed in following Chapeter \ref{Chapter: models specs}.

Both architectures were configured as \emph{multi-task classifiers}, jointly predicting two distinct output targets from a shared representation of the input flow:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Binary task} (\texttt{label\_generic}): the model predicts whether a flow is benign ($0$) or malicious ($1$), irrespective of the specific attack category. This task is analogous to anomaly detection: the classifier must identify any deviation from normal traffic behaviour.
    \item \textbf{Multi-class task} (\texttt{Label}): the model simultaneously predicts the specific traffic category (Benign or one of 14 mutually exclusive attack types), requiring the classifier to learn fine-grained discriminative representations that distinguish between attack subtypes with often similar flow-level statistics.
\end{itemize}

The joint training of both tasks on a shared encoder has two advantages.  First, it allows the binary and multi-class signals to act as complementary supervisory gradients, potentially regularising the shared representation and improving generalization.  Second, it makes it possible to detect \emph{asymmetric} effects of data corruption: noise that does not materially degrade binary detection may nonetheless destroy the classifier's ability to resolve attack subtypes, a phenomenon that would be invisible if only one task were monitored.

\subsubsection{Tabnet}
\label{Chapter: model tabnet}
% =======================================================================

TabNet\supercite{arik2021tabnet} is a deep learning architecture explicitly designed for tabular data, proposed by Arik and Pfister at Google Cloud AI.  Its distinctive mechanism is \emph{sequential attention}: at each of $N_{\text{steps}}$ decision steps, a learnable attention mask selects a sparse subset of input features to process, and the outputs of successive steps are aggregated to produce the final prediction.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{images/tabnet.png}
    \caption{TabNet architecture, composed of feature transformer, attentive transformer and feature masking at each decision step. Split block divides the processed representation into two, to be used at the attentive transformer of the subsequent step and to be used towards construction of functionality, and the masks can be aggregated (using the Agg. block) ultimately to obtain global the overall output. At each decision step, its feature selection mask can provide insights about its feature important attribution behavior. (b) A feature transformer block example – 4-layer network is shown, where 2 of them are shared across all decision steps and 2 of them are decision step-dependent. (c) An attentive transformer block example – a single layer mapping is modulated with a prior scale information, which aggregates how much each feature has been used before the current decision step. Normalization of the coefficients is employed using sparsemax (Martins \& Astudillo, 2016).}
    \label{fig:tabnet-arhitecture}
\end{figure}

Key architectural components include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feature transformer}: a shared-across-steps and step-dependent stack of fully connected layers with batch normalisation and Gated Linear Units (GLU).
    \item \textbf{Attentive transformer}: a sparsemax or entmax activated attention mechanism that enforces feature sparsity at each step, only a small number of features receive non-zero attention.
    \item \textbf{Sparsity regularisation}: a coefficient $\lambda_{\text{sparse}}$ penalises the entropy of the attention distribution, encouraging the  model to rely on a compact set of features.
\end{itemize}

TabNet's attention masks are directly interpretable: by averaging the masks over all instances and steps, a global feature importance ranking is obtained without any post-hoc explanation technique.  This property is especially valuable in the context of noise-injection experiments, as it allows tracking \emph{how} the model redistributes its attention when specific features are corrupted.

In this work, TabNet is instantiated as a \texttt{TabNetMultiTaskClassifier} from the \texttt{pytorch-tabnet} library, with two prediction heads sharing a common encoder: one for the binary label (\texttt{label\_generic}) and one for the multi-class label (\texttt{Label}).
Hyper-parameters including: the attention dimension, the number of steps, the relaxation factor, the sparsity coefficient, the learning rate, batch size, and mask type are all tuned via Optuna with a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler, optimising the mean Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) across both tasks.

\subsubsection{TFT \& Problems with this model}
\label{Chapter: model TFT}
% =======================================================================

The Temporal Fusion Transformer is a transformer-based architecture developed at Google for interpretable multi-horizon time-series forecasting. Its architecture comprises several specialized sub-networks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Variable Selection Networks (VSNs)}: GRN-based (Gated Residual Network) modules that assign learned importance weights to each input variable, independently for the encoder (past inputs) and the decoder (known future inputs).  VSNs provide per-time-step feature importance, enabling the identification of which features are informative and \emph{when}.
    \item \textbf{Static covariate encoders}: networks that process time-invariant metadata and inject it as context into all time-dependent components, enriching the representation with entity-level information.
    \item \textbf{Temporal self-attention}: a multi-head attention layer over the encoder's temporal dimension, allowing the model to capture long-range dependencies across time steps.
    \item \textbf{Gated skip connections and layer normalization}: used throughout the architecture to facilitate gradient flow and prevent overfitting.
\end{itemize}

Initially, this thesis evaluated TFT as a plausible candidate for a sequential classification model as network intrusion detection. The chronologically ordered network flows were treated as a single continuous time series, and the model was asked to predict the binary and multi-class labels of the current flow given an encoder window of the preceding flows, exploiting the temporal patterns characteristic of attack campaigns (e.g., the bursty structure of DDoS floods or the sequential probing of port scans). This, accompanied by the possibility to natively extract the feature importance scores made this a very interesting candidate. Yet it was discarded has 

\paragraph{Structural incompatibility with the classification task.}
Despite the theoretical appeal, the TFT architecture presents a fundamental mismatch with the flow-level classification objective.  TFT is designed around three distinct input streams: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{static entity covariates} (time-invariant metadata associated with each series)
    \item \emph{known future inputs} (covariates whose values at future time steps are available at inference time, such as calendar features in demand forecasting)
    \item \emph{past observed inputs} (the historical measurements being modelled)
\end{itemize}
The CIC-IDS2018 dataset does not instantiate any of these streams naturally: it consists of aggregated per-flow statistics in which each row is a self-contained measurement with no associated entity metadata and no known future covariate.  The absence of future inputs in particular forces TFT to operate exclusively on its encoder pathway, effectively degrading it to a more complex but less appropriate version of a standard sequence encoder.

\paragraph{Empirical evidence of failure.}
The structural mismatch was confirmed empirically during preliminary experiments.  The trained models consistently collapsed their predictions to a single dominant class, producing confusion matrices with all entries concentrated in one column and yielding Matthews Correlation Coefficients near zero for both tasks.  The Variable Selection Network weights were also uninformative, assigning near-uniform importance to all features, a symptom of the model failing to identify discriminative temporal structure.  These outcomes are not attributable to hyperparameter misconfiguration but rather to the fundamental unsuitability of the forecasting objective for a retrospective classification setting.

\paragraph{An additional limitation: the inter-flow independence assumption.}
A further structural issue specific to the CIC-IDS2018 dataset concerns the ordering of rows.  CICFlowMeter aggregates each network flow individually and stores flows from all active connections interleaved by arrival time.  Consequently, two chronologically adjacent rows are frequently from entirely different source-destination pairs rather than from the same ongoing session.  For example, a DoS flow from host A at time $t$ may be immediately followed by a benign flow from host B at time $t{+}\delta$, then by another DoS flow from host A at time $t{+}2\delta$.  A model that treats this sequence as a coherent temporal signal will attempt to learn transitions between flows that have no causal or temporal relationship, introducing spurious noise into the temporal representation.  While this limitation also affects the CNN-LSTM model to some degree, the latter is far less sensitive to it because its convolutional front-end primarily captures local \emph{statistical} regularities across the window (e.g., a sustained elevation in packet counts across 50 flows), rather than relying on precise causal ordering between individual pairs of adjacent rows as TFT's attention mechanism implicitly does.

For the reasons outlined above, TFT was ultimately excluded from the experimental pipeline, and the CNN-LSTM architecture described in the following section was adopted as the time-series model for all subsequent experiments.

\subsubsection{CNN-LSTM}
\label{Chapter: model cnn-lstm}
% =======================================================================

The CNN-LSTM model\supercite{jablaoui2024effective} is a custom multi-task architecture that overcomes the limitations of TFT described above by combining a one-dimensional convolutional front-end with a stacked LSTM backbone, operating directly on fixed-length temporal windows of chronologically ordered flows. Its design philosophy is well suited to the flow-level IDS classification task: rather than modelling precise causal transitions between individual flow pairs, it extracts statistical regularities within compact windows of consecutive flows, a signal that is both meaningful and robust to the inter-flow independence issue noted in previous chapter.

The fundamental assumption underpinning the CNN-LSTM's use of temporal structure is that attack campaigns leave identifiable \emph{aggregate} signatures across short windows of consecutive flows, even when those flows do not originate from the same source-destination pair.  A volumetric DDoS flood, for instance, raises the mean packet count, byte rate, and flow density across any 50-flow window captured during the attack period, regardless of whether those 50 flows all belong to the same attacker.  Similarly, a port-scan campaign produces a characteristic elevation of the number of distinct destination ports observed within a window, even when individual flows are interleaved with benign traffic.  These window-level statistical signatures are precisely the patterns that the CNN and LSTM components are designed to detect.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{images/CNN-LSTM-structure.png}
    \caption{CNN-LSTM-structur}
    \label{fig:CNN-LSTM-structure}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Temporal window construction.}
The model receives a three-dimensional input tensor of shape $(B \times T \times F)$, where $B$ is the batch size, $T = 50$ is the window length (number of consecutive flows), and $F$ is the number of input features.  Each sample is constructed by a sliding-window procedure applied to the chronologically sorted flow sequence, with a stride of $S = 10$ flows.  The label assigned to each window is that of its \emph{last} (most recent) flow, consistent with a causal streaming scenario in which a detection decision is issued after observing the most recent packet in the context of the preceding $T-1$ packets:

\[
    X^{(3D)}[j] = X^{(2D)}[jS : jS + T], \quad y^{(3D)}[j] = y^{(2D)}[jS + T - 1].
\]

If the flows were not sorted chronologically, the windows would contain temporally unrelated packets and the temporal reasoning capacity of the model would be entirely negated.
\paragraph{Model Structure:}
\begin{itemize}

\item{Convolutional front-end: local pattern extraction.}
The input is first permuted to $(B \times F \times T)$ so that each feature dimension acts as a channel.  A single \texttt{Conv1d} layer with $n_{\text{filters}}$ output channels and kernel size $k$ (with symmetric zero-padding to preserve the temporal dimension) slides \emph{along the time axis}, computing local dot products between the kernel and groups of $k$ consecutive flow vectors.  This operation is sensitive to short-range temporal regularities: a sudden elevation in packet counts sustained over $k$ consecutive flows (as in the onset of a DDoS burst), or a rapid sequential increase in destination port values (as in a port scan), will produce distinctive activation patterns in specific filter channels.  The output is passed through ReLU activation, \texttt{MaxPool1d} with kernel size 2 (halving the temporal resolution and retaining the dominant local activations), and \texttt{BatchNorm1d} (stabilising the distribution of activations across the batch and accelerating convergence).

\item{LSTM backbone: long-range temporal dependencies.}
The pooled convolutional output is permuted back to $(B \times T/2 \times n_{\text{filters}})$ and fed sequentially into two stacked LSTM layers with hidden dimensions $h_1$ and $h_2$ respectively.  LSTM cells maintain an explicit \emph{cell state} and a \emph{hidden state} across time steps, updated through input, forget, and output gates.  This allows the model to selectively retain information from earlier positions in the window (e.g., the onset of an elevated-traffic phase) while discarding irrelevant transitions (e.g., a single anomalous flow embedded in otherwise benign traffic).  The two-layer stacking enables the second LSTM to learn higher-order temporal abstractions from the sequential representations produced by the first.  Only the final hidden state (the representation at the last time step, $\mathbf{h}_{T/2}$) is extracted and passed downstream, providing a fixed-length summary of the entire window's temporal dynamics.

\item{Classification heads.}
A shared fully connected block (linear layer, ReLU, Dropout($p$)) maps the context vector to a common representation, from which two independent linear projection heads produce the $n_{\text{bin}} = 2$ logits for the binary task and the $n_{\text{mul}}$ logits for the multi-class task.  The combined training loss is the sum of the two cross-entropy losses.  Gradient clipping (max norm $= 1.0$) is applied at every backward pass to prevent gradient explosion in the LSTM cells, and a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler (reduction factor $= 0.5$, patience $= 3$ epochs) adapts the learning rate to the validation loss trajectory.  Early stopping (patience $= 10$ epochs) prevents overfitting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Sample Dimensionality: a Structural Asymmetry}
\label{sec:sample-dimensionality}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

An important practical consequence of the two models' distinct input representations is that, for identical underlying data, the number of samples available for training and evaluation differs substantially between TabNet and CNN-LSTM.

\textbf{TabNet} operates on 2D data: every flow record constitutes an independent sample.  On the 10\% stratified sub-dataset, the validation partition therefore contains approximately $269\,000$ samples, one per flow.

\textbf{CNN-LSTM} operates on 3D data: consecutive flows are grouped into sliding windows of width $T = 50$ with stride $S = 10$.  Each window yields exactly one prediction (that of its final flow), so the effective number of validation samples is:
\[
    N_{\text{windows}} = \left\lfloor \frac{N_{\text{val}} - T}{S} \right\rfloor + 1
    \approx \frac{N_{\text{val}}}{S} \approx 27\,000.
\]

The resulting approximately 10:1 ratio between TabNet and CNN-LSTM validation set sizes (summarised in Table~\ref{tab:sample-sizes}) is an inherent consequence of the windowing design and is by construction, not an artefact of unequal data access.  Its practical implication is that evaluation metrics for CNN-LSTM are computed over a substantially smaller sample than those for TabNet, which must be taken into account when comparing the magnitude and stability of observed performance differences across experiments.

\label{tab:sample-sizes}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrl}

\textbf{Model}  & \textbf{Validation samples} & \textbf{Sampling unit} \\
\midrule
TabNet          & $\approx$269\,000           & 1 flow = 1 sample \\

\end{table}

\section{Hyperparameter Optimization}
\label{sec:hpo}
% ============================================================

Hyperparameter optimization was conducted independently for TabNet and CNN-LSTM using Optuna with the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler and the Median Pruner. This practice was done in the same exact way for both \red{Experiment A} and \red{Experiment B}. The primary design requirement was to obtain well-calibrated hyperparameters without incurring the computational cost of training on the full dataset: accordingly, HPO was performed on a \textbf{0.1\% stratified random sample} of the 2 previously defined versions of the CIC-IDS2018 (discussed in Section \ref{sec:reduced-feature-set}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item For \red{Experiment A}: it was used a stratified 0.1\% of the 44 feature reduced dataset.
    \item For \red{Experiment B}: it was used a stratified 0.1\% of the full 79 feature dataset.
\end{itemize}
Doing this, both resulting dataset yielded approximately 16\,000 flow-packet-records. For both Experiments, this sample was divided using the same temporal splitting strategy described in Section \ref{sec:temporal-split}, so that the relative ordering of flows was preserved even at this reduced scale.

The objective function for TabNet was the \emph{mean MCC} across both tasks:
\[
    \mathcal{O}_{\text{TabNet}} = \frac{\mathrm{MCC}_{\text{bin}} + \mathrm{MCC}_{\text{mul}}}{2},
\]
while for CNN-LSTM, because the validation loss is already monitored internally for early stopping and pruning, the objective function returned to Optuna was the \emph{mean F1-score} across both tasks (macro average for the multi-class task):
\[
    \mathcal{O}_{\text{CNN-LSTM}} = \frac{F1_{\text{bin}} + F1_{\text{mul}}}{2}.
\]

This was done because, as we've previously seen in Chapter \ref{sec:label-distribution}, the dataset is heavily imbalanced, therefore basing our objective functions on maximizing accuracy could lead the models to alaways predict the majority class. Both metrics deployed: MCC and F1-Score, were used as those metric are much more reliable when dataset is imbalanced giving heavy importance even to the fewer represented label classes.

Both objectives were maximized. For CNN-LSTM the Median Pruner was configured to discard unpromising trials after a warm-up period of five trials and five internal report steps, allowing early termination of trials whose intermediate metrics fell below the median of all completed trials at the same step.

\subsection{Batch Size Scaling}
\label{sec:batch-scaling}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

A practical challenge arising from HPO on a $0.1\%$ sample is that the optimal batch size identified on the small sample may be suboptimal when training on the $10\%$ dataset used for training Baseline models and each of the many models trained for \red{Experiment A} and \red{Experiment B}. To address this, the linear scaling rule (He et al.) was applied:
\[
    B_{\text{scaled}} = \min\!\Bigl( B_{\text{HPO}} \cdot \frac{0.10}{0.001},\; 4096 \Bigr),
\]
capping the scaled batch size at 4\,096 to avoid excessive GPU memory consumption and prevent the per-batch gradient estimates from becoming excessively noisy at very large sizes.

For TabNet, the sparsity regularisation coefficient $\lambda_{\text{sparse}}$ was also rescaled according to:
\[
    \lambda_{\text{scaled}} = \lambda_{\text{HPO}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{0.001}{0.10}},
\]
a conservative correction that accounts for the increase in effective gradient magnitude at larger batch sizes while preventing over-regularisation on the larger dataset.

For CNN-LSTM, the learning rate was halved relative to the HPO value to ensure stable convergence at the higher data volume, following common practice in large-batch training protocols.

\section{Feature Set Selection for Baselines and Experiments Training}
\label{sec:feature-selection}
% ============================================================

A central design choice of the experimental protocol is the selection of the feature set on which both baseline models and all noise-injection experiments operate.  Rather than using the full reduced feature set described in Section \ref{sec:reduced-feature-set} (44 features for Experiment A, 79 for Experiment~B), a compact set of \textbf{9/10 features} was constructed for the baseline experiments through a principled importance-based selection procedure. This compact set serves three purposes: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item it makes the computational cost of training both models across \emph{thousands} of noise-injection experiments tractable, as doing all experiments requires both a lot of computational power and time;
    \item it ensures that the features selected for corruption are directly linked to the models' predictive behaviour; 
    \item it provides a controlled experimental setting in which the contribution of each feature can be assessed in isolation.
\end{itemize}

Yet, since the main goal of the 2 experiments is very different, tow diverse subsets of feature were selected in this phase. 

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Feature Importance Extraction}
\label{sec:fi-extraction}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

After identifying the best trial for each model, a common step done for both experiments every feature importance scores were extracted using the two mechanisms natively available in the respective architectures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TabNet:} the library exposes \texttt{feature\_importances\_}, an array representing the mean attention mask weight assigned to each feature across all decision steps and all validation samples.  Features with a high average attention weight are those most frequently selected by the sparse attention mechanism, and can therefore be interpreted as carrying the most discriminative information for both tasks jointly.
    \item \textbf{CNN-LSTM:} since the architecture does not expose a native feature importance measure, therefore \emph{permutation importance} was employed.  For each feature $i$, the values along feature dimension 2 of the entire validation tensor were randomly permuted across samples (while leaving all other features intact) and the resulting drop in binary classification accuracy, $\Delta_i = \mathrm{acc}_{\text{base}} - \mathrm{acc}_{\text{permuted},i}$, was recorded.  The raw $\Delta_i$ values were normalised to $[0, 1]$ by dividing by the maximum observed drop, yielding a relative importance score $\hat{\Delta}_i \in [0, 1]$.  Negative drops (i.e.\ permuting the feature actually improved accuracy) were clipped to zero.
\end{itemize}

Both models were thus endowed with a ranked list of features ordered by their respective importance scores.

\section{Baseline Model Training}
\label{sec:baseline-training}
% ============================================================

The baseline-models constitute the reference against which every noise-injection experiment is evaluated. Therefore, just as the noise injection experiments, They were trained on a \textbf{10\% stratified sample} of the CIC-IDS2018 dataset (stratified by \texttt{label\_generic}) using the nine features selected in Section~\ref{sec:feature-selection} and the hyperparameters from the best HPO trials described in Section~\ref{sec:hpo}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Temporal Dataset Split}
\label{sec:temporal-split}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

A fundamental requirement of the experimental design is that the train/validation/test partition \emph{preserves the chronological ordering} of packet-flows. Shuffling the data before splitting would allow flows from the same temporal burst to completely change position, thus completely losing temporal meaning. While this would represent no problem at all for the TabNet model (as this model is deployed to analyze the dataset as tabular data, therefore the model doesn't care about packet ordering) it would represent an huge problem for the CNN-LSTM model (as this model is deployed in order to analyze the dataset as a time-series). For this particular reason it was  crucial to keep original data flow intact(in order to explicitly exploits temporal context through its sliding-window input representation). 

At first glance one could think to just use a smaller sample of days of the whole 5 monitored inside CIC-IDS2018 dataset. Yet, this option was quickly discarded, as not all attack types manifested inside all day and having a restricted set of days meant forcibly delete some error classes, which is a side effect we wanted to avoid.   

In order to solve both problems (keeping temporal structure intact, while also keeping all error classes) it was adopted a split strategy (entirely \emph{shuffle-free}) that proceeded as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Flow-Packet-records are sorted in ascending order of \texttt{Timestamp}.
    \item Of the original $\approx$16 million CIC-IDS2018 flow-packet-record only 1 in 10 is maintained. The union of retained packets form the $\approx$1.6 million rows dataset from where train/test/val-sets are extracted. This could seem like a big cut from the original dataset but when you consider that in today's standards a regular application sends hundreds, if not thousands, of web-packets in a few seconds (of wich some inevitably get lost in the web routing); even cutting the original dataset to $1/10$ of it's original size should still keep intact it's syntactical and semantical meaning (registering no particular loss of information).
    \item From previously computed dataset, a group index is assigned to each flow-packet-record: $g_i = i \bmod 3$, where $i$ is the 0-based position in the sorted sequence.
    \item Flows with $g_i \in \{0, 1\}$ (two thirds of the data) form the \emph{training set}.
    \item Flows with $g_i = 2$ (one third, the \emph{temporary set}) are further divided in an interleaved fashion: even-positioned temporary flows ($\text{pos} \bmod 2 = 0$) go to the \emph{validation set}, and odd-positioned flows go to the \emph{test set}.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure is summarized in Fig \ref{fig:dataset_split} below:

\section{Experimental Grid: Full Overview}
\label{sec:experiment-protocol}
% ============================================================

With the baselines established, we can now discuss the main experimental pipeline. The principal experimental campaign was executed following a fully automated pipeline that iterates over all combinations of noise injection method, target column, corruption percentage, and random seed, retraining both TabNet and CNN-LSTM from scratch for each combination using the best hyperparameters and feature-sets identified in Section \ref{sec:hpo} and also using datasets that only retain both:
\begin{itemize}
    \item fewer features (9/10 features as defined in Section \ref{sec:combined-ranking})
    \item fewer samples (only 10\% of total dataset, using technique defined in Section \ref{sec:temporal-split})
\end{itemize}
Both of those reductions in features and samples were necessary in order to process the enormous amount of experiments we are about to cover.

Inside this very own Chapter introduction (Chapter \ref{chap:5-Empiric-analysis-models}) it were given the full explanations of both Experiment A and B final goal. Those Experiments constitute the analytical backbone of this thesis and their ultimate goal can be summarized as follows: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \red{Experiment A}: aims to create a proof of concept that it is possible to increase deep learning based IDSs performances by applying targeted noise injection in training phase. 
    \item \red{Experiment B}: deals with the much more theoretical aspects, the aim of this Expriment collection is to have an in depth study of full possible variations and effects that noise injection could unleash to a tabular-datest and a time-series. This is possible through exploitation of CIC-IDS2018 as both: a tabular dataset and a time-series of network-flow-packets.
\end{itemize}

This section quantifies precisely the grid of each phase, since the two phases differ in the dataset employed, the set of corrupted columns, the percentage grid, and the number of random seeds. Throughout this section, a single \emph{experiment} denotes one fully specified configuration consistent of: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item One designated feature, or feature group.
    \item One of the 5 noise methods applicable by the \texttt{Pucktrick} library, previously discussed in Chapter \ref{sec:method_description}.
    \item One of 6/7 (depending on Experiment-A / Experiment B) corruption percentages passed to selected \texttt{Pucktrick} method to inject noise in the dataset. 
    \item One of the 5/20 random seeds used for that particular Experiment branch. Each random seed deterministically governs the stratified sampling, the temporal split ordering, the \texttt{Pucktrick} noise injection realization, and the PyTorch and Lightning random states.  Repeating the full grid over multiple seeds is what permits the construction of Student-$t$ confidence intervals around the observed performance deltas in Chapter~\ref{chap:Empiric-analysis-models}, ensuring that the reported effects are statistically robust and not artifacts of a particular random split or noise realization.
\end{itemize}
Another thing to consider is that every such experiment trains \emph{both} models: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item TabNet: Trained using the dataset described in Section \ref{sec:temporal-split}.
    \item CNN-LSTM: Trained using the same dataset but divided in time windows as described in Section \ref{sec:sample-dimensionality}.
\end{itemize}
Therefore the number of trained models is always twice the number of experiment configurations.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Noise Injection and Dataset Construction per Experiment}
\label{sec:noise-construction}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Before stating final amount of total experiments, this chapter This chapter covers the dataset construction and noise injection pipeline used for \emph{each} experiment: 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The full 10\%-sampled Spark DataFrame is loaded from the Parquet archive and restricted to the nine important features plus the mandatory columns (\texttt{Label}, \texttt{label\_generic}, \texttt{Timestamp}).
    \item The data is ordered chronologically and divided into training, validation, and test partitions using the temporal split described in Section~\ref{sec:temporal-split}.  The \emph{validation and test partitions remain entirely clean} throughout: only the training partition is subjected to PuckTrick noise injection.
    \item A PuckTrick \texttt{OBJ} is instantiated, and the selected corruption method from newly developed Spark-Backend is invoked with a strategy dictionary specifying the target feature, the desired corruption percentage $p$, and the \texttt{"new"} mode (corruption applied from zero).
    \item The corrupted training DataFrame is converted to Pandas, preprocessed with the same label encoder fitted on the global sample, scaled with the same \texttt{StandardScaler} fitted on the clean training partition, and assembled into 2D and 3D arrays.
    \item Rare classes (fewer than five samples in the training set) are filtered and the multi-class label index space is remapped to a contiguous integer range $[0, n_{\text{classes}}-1]$, ensuring that the CrossEntropyLoss does not encounter out-of-range target indices.
\end{enumerate}

This design guarantees that each experiment measures exclusively the effect of corrupting the training data: the evaluation is always performed on the same clean held-out validation flows, making all performance numbers directly comparable across experiments and seeds.

\subsection{Experiment A: Proof of Concept}
\label{sec:exp-A-grid}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Experiment-A is the smaller, proof-of-concept phase, executed on the feature-pruned dataset described in Section \ref{sec:combined-ranking}.  Its objective is to demonstrate that controlled noise injection \emph{can} improve IDS model performance; it is therefore deliberately limited in scope and was repeated over \textbf{5 random seeds}:
\[
    S_{A} = \{1,\; 2,\; 42,\; 51,\; 84\}.
\]

The corrupted columns, target for this specific experiments, are a sub-set of \textbf{4 columns} drawn from the reduced feature set, chosen to span distinct feature types and fragility levels: \texttt{FIN Flag Cnt} (binary), \texttt{Down/Up Ratio} (continuous), \texttt{Protocol} (categorical), and \texttt{Timestamp} (temporal, used as the row-level reference for \texttt{duplicated}).  The percentage grid comprises \textbf{7 levels}:
\[
    p_{A} \in \{1\%,\; 5\%,\; 10\%,\; 20\%,\; 30\%,\; 50\%,\; 75\%\}.
\]

The experimentation pipeline executes two nested loops, which must be summed to obtain the per-seed count.  The first loop sweeps the four feature-level methods over the four columns; however, the \texttt{labels} method is skipped on columns that are neither binary nor categorical (\texttt{Down/Up Ratio} and \texttt{Timestamp}), so it contributes only $2$ of the $4$ columns.  The second loop applies the row-level \texttt{duplicated} method once, over the same $7$ percentages.  Formally, the per-seed configuration count is:

\begin{align*}
N_{A}^{\text{seed}}
&= \underbrace{\bigl( \underbrace{3}_{\substack{\text{missing,}\\\text{outliers, noise}}} \times \underbrace{4}_{\text{cols}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{labels}} \times \underbrace{2}_{\substack{\text{binary/}\\\text{categ.\ cols}}} \bigr) \times \underbrace{7}_{\text{pct}}}_{\text{loop 1: feature-level methods}}
   \;+\; \underbrace{\underbrace{1}_{\text{duplicated}} \times \underbrace{1}_{\text{Timestamp}} \times \underbrace{7}_{\text{pct}}}_{\text{loop 2: row-level method}} \\[4pt]
&= (12 + 2)\times 7 \;+\; 7
 \;=\; 98 + 7 \;=\; \mathbf{105}.
\end{align*}

Multiplying by the $5$ random seeds and by the $2$ models trained per configuration gives the total cost of Experiment~A:
\[
    N_{A}^{\text{total}} = 105 \times 5 = \mathbf{525} \text{ configurations} \;\Longrightarrow\; 525 \times 2 = \mathbf{1\,050} \text{ trained models}.
\]

\subsection{Experiment B: Main Focus}
\label{sec:exp-B-grid}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Experiment~B is the principal analytical phase of the thesis.  It operates on the complete dataset (no features discarded prior to HPO, although every experiment is conducted on the nine-feature working set derived in Section~\ref{sec:feature-selection}) and was repeated over a far broader range of \textbf{20 random seeds}:
\[
    S_{B} = \{1, 11, 21, 31, 41, 42, 51, 61, 86, 101, 202, 303, 404, 505, 606, 707, 808, 909, 1010, 1111\}.
\]
All Experiment~B runs use the \textbf{6-level} percentage grid:
\[
    p_{B} \in \{5\%,\; 10\%,\; 20\%,\; 35\%,\; 50\%,\; 75\%\}.
\]

Experiment~B is itself articulated into two complementary scenarios: \emph{single-feature} corruption (one column corrupted at a time, Section~\ref{sec:exp-B-single}) and \emph{multiple-feature} corruption (several columns corrupted simultaneously, Section~\ref{sec:exp-B-multi}).  Both scenarios share the seed set $S_{B}$ and the percentage grid $p_{B}$ defined above.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Single-feature corruption}
\label{sec:exp-B-single}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

In the single-feature scenario noise is injected into one column at a time.  It is realised by two distinct scripts, both of which corrupt a single column per run but target different kinds of columns; their counts are therefore reported in separate paragraphs and summed:

\paragraph{Feature corruption:}
This script corrupts individual \emph{input features}, sweeping the nine-feature working set plus the \texttt{Timestamp} column (\textbf{10 columns}, wich consist of all the columns listed in Section \ref{sec:combined-ranking}). Like Experiment-A, it executes two nested loops.  The first loop sweeps the four feature-level methods over the 10 columns, with \texttt{labels} restricted to discrete columns; within this working set it was applied only on \texttt{Protocol}, not because it was the only one that qualified for this as there were some more other valid candidate features that were either binary or categorical, but because we wanted to limit the number of experiment total to the sole necessary. Therefore \texttt{labels} contributes a single feature column (but this \texttt{Puckrick} method will be further explored in next paragraph). The second loop applies \texttt{duplicated} once at the row level on \texttt{Timestamp}.  Over the $6$ percentages of $p_{B}$:

\begin{align*}
N_{B,\text{feat}}^{\text{seed}}
&= \underbrace{\bigl( \underbrace{3}_{\substack{\text{missing,}\\\text{outliers, noise}}} \times \underbrace{10}_{\text{cols}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{labels}} \times \underbrace{1}_{\text{Protocol}} \bigr) \times \underbrace{6}_{\text{pct}}}_{\text{loop 1: feature-level methods}}
   \;+\; \underbrace{\underbrace{1}_{\text{duplicated}} \times \underbrace{1}_{\text{Timestamp}} \times \underbrace{6}_{\text{pct}}}_{\text{loop 2: row-level method}} \\[4pt]
&= (30 + 1)\times 6 \;+\; 6 \;=\; 186 + 6 \;=\; \mathbf{192} \quad\text{per seed.}
\end{align*}

\paragraph{Target-label corruption:}
A complementary script applies the \texttt{labels} method directly to the two encoded \emph{target} columns rather than to input features: the multi-class target \texttt{Label} and the binary target \texttt{label\_generic} (the list \texttt{Y\_TARGET\_COLUMNS\_TO\_DIRTY}).  This isolates the effect of label noise on the supervision signal itself, a scenario distinct from corrupting an input feature.  Because PuckTrick's \texttt{labels} method requires an integer column, label encoding is performed \emph{before} noise injection.  A single loop sweeps the $2$ target columns over the $6$ percentages:
\[
    N_{B,\text{targ}}^{\text{seed}}
    = \underbrace{1}_{\text{labels}} \times \underbrace{2}_{\substack{\texttt{Label},\\\texttt{label\_generic}}} \times \underbrace{6}_{\text{pct}}
    = \mathbf{12} \quad\text{per seed.}
\]

\paragraph{Single-feature subtotal.}
Summing the two scripts and scaling over the 20 seeds and the 2 models per configuration:
\[
    N_{B,\text{single}}^{\text{total}} = \bigl(192 + 12\bigr) \times 20 = 204 \times 20 = \mathbf{4\,080} \text{ configurations}
    \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{8\,160} \text{ trained models}.
\]

\subsubsection{Multiple-feature corruption}
\label{sec:exp-B-multi}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The multiple-feature scenario corrupts \emph{several columns simultaneously} within a single PuckTrick call, in order to study compound degradation effects that the single-feature experiments cannot capture.  The script iterates over a list of column groups; the groups split naturally into two categories: groups of input features and the group of target columns. Those two groups use different methods and are therefore counted in two separate paragraphs and summed.

\paragraph{Feature-group corruption.}
Six groups of \emph{input features} are corrupted with the three feature-level methods (\texttt{missing}, \texttt{outliers}, \texttt{noise}); the \texttt{duplicated} method is intentionally excluded, since duplicating whole rows is already equivalent to corrupting all features at once (already covered by the single-feature \texttt{duplicated} run); while \texttt{labels} is purposefully excluded as it will be explored in the following paragraph. The six selected groups are designed to probe different combinations of feature importance and fragility listed below:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \{\texttt{Fwd Act Data Pkts}, \texttt{Dst Port}, \texttt{Init Fwd Win Byts}\}: the three most important features;
    \item \{\texttt{Protocol}, \texttt{PSH Flag Cnt}, \texttt{Fwd Seg Size Min}\}: the middle-ranked, type-diverse features;
    \item \{\texttt{Bwd Byts/b Avg}, \texttt{Fwd Blk Rate Avg}, \texttt{Idle Mean}\}: the three least important features (negative control);
    \item \{\texttt{Fwd Act Data Pkts}, \texttt{PSH Flag Cnt}, \texttt{Idle Mean}\}: one feature drawn from each importance tier;
    \item \{\texttt{Fwd Act Data Pkts}, \texttt{Dst Port}, \texttt{Init Fwd Win Byts}, \texttt{Protocol}, \texttt{PSH Flag Cnt}, \texttt{Fwd Seg Size Min}\}: the combined top and middle tiers (6 features);
    \item \{\texttt{Fwd Act Data Pkts}, \texttt{Idle Mean}, \texttt{Init Fwd Win Byts}\}: two high-fragility features plus one medium-fragility feature (where fragility measure come from Section \ref{sec:fragility}).
\end{enumerate}

Over the $6$ percentages of $p_{B}$, the feature-group loop yields:
\[
    N_{B,\text{grp}}^{\text{seed}}
    = \underbrace{6}_{\text{groups}} \times \underbrace{3}_{\substack{\text{missing,}\\\text{outliers, noise}}} \times \underbrace{6}_{\text{pct}}
    = \mathbf{108} \quad\text{per seed.}
\]

A seventh group bundles the two target columns \{\texttt{Label}, \texttt{label\_generic}\} and corrupts them \emph{jointly} with the \texttt{labels} method in a single call, the multi-target analogue of the target-label script of Section~\ref{sec:exp-B-single}. A single loop sweeps this one group over the $6$ percentages:
\[
    N_{B,\text{lblgrp}}^{\text{seed}}
    = \underbrace{1}_{\text{group}} \times \underbrace{1}_{\text{labels}} \times \underbrace{6}_{\text{pct}}
    = \mathbf{6} \quad\text{per seed.}
\]

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Computational Infrastructure}
\label{sec:infra}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Local development and prototyping (including hyperparameter optimisation and baseline training) were carried out on a single workstation with a consumer GPU.  The full experiment grid, comprising several thousand training runs across both \red{Experiment A} and \red{Experiment B}, was executed partially on same local machine and partially on an HPC cluster equipped with dedicated GPU nodes. A per-process GPU memory limit of 80\% to avoid out-of-memory errors during concurrent GPU-accelerated model evaluation.  Because out-of-memory crashes are a recurrent risk in long unattended runs, a watchdog process monitored each job and automatically restarted it from the most recent persisted checkpoint upon failure.
