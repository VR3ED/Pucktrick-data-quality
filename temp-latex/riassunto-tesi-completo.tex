\section{Motivation, Context, and Objectives}
% =====================================================================

The quality of training data is one of the most critical determinants of the
performance and reliability of deep learning models. Real-world datasets are
seldom pristine: they are pervasively affected by missing values, mislabelled
instances, duplicate records, noisy measurements, and statistical outliers,
arising from sensor malfunction, human annotation error, or data integration.
When such corrupted data are consumed by learning algorithms, the resulting
models may exhibit degraded generalisation, biased predictions, or, in
safety-critical domains such as cybersecurity, harmful decision-making.

Within the paradigm of \emph{Data-Centric AI}, systematic curation of the
training data takes precedence over model-architecture design, and central to
this paradigm is the ability to quantify the impact of specific data-quality
issues through controlled, reproducible perturbations. \textbf{PuckTrick} is an
open-source Python library developed at the University of Milano-Bicocca that
addresses precisely this need, providing a unified programmatic interface for
the controlled injection of five fundamental categories of data corruption into
tabular datasets: \emph{duplicated records}, \emph{missing values}, \emph{noisy
values}, \emph{outliers}, and \emph{label inconsistencies}. In its original form
PuckTrick operates exclusively on Pandas DataFrames, which become a bottleneck
when datasets scale to millions of rows: a limitation that motivates its
extension to distributed computing.

The empirical component of this thesis is situated within \emph{network
intrusion detection}. The dataset selected is the \textbf{CSE-CIC-IDS2018},
a publicly available benchmark comprising over \textbf{16 million} labelled
network-flow instances spanning benign traffic and fourteen attack categories,
collected over a multi-day capture window in a controlled cyber-range. This
thesis therefore pursues two interrelated objectives:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Objective 1 --- Extending PuckTrick to Apache Spark.} The
        primary engineering contribution is the extension of PuckTrick to
        support Apache Spark (via PySpark) as an alternative backend alongside
        Pandas, carried out in the \texttt{Spark\_Conversion} branch in
        collaboration with Daniel Satriano and under the supervision of
        Professor Andrea Maurino.
  \item \textbf{Objective 2 --- Evaluating controlled noise on deep learning
        NIDS.} An empirical investigation into the effect of PuckTrick-generated
        corruption on two complementary models, evaluated at two levels of
        granularity: a \emph{binary} task (benign vs.\ malicious, analogous to
        anomaly detection) and a \emph{multi-class} task (the specific attack
        type, a substantially harder problem).
\end{itemize}

Studying both levels simultaneously is essential because corruption may have
asymmetric effects. Two state-of-the-art architectures were selected, both
configured as \emph{multi-task classifiers} predicting the binary and the
multi-class label from a shared representation: \textbf{TabNet}, a sequential
attention-based architecture for tabular data with natively interpretable
feature importance, and \textbf{CNN-LSTM}, a convolutional backbone augmented
with LSTM blocks that learns from chronologically ordered time windows of flows.

The empirical work is organised into two branches: \textbf{Experiment~A}
(a proof of concept on a feature-pruned \emph{reduced} dataset, over 5 random
seeds) demonstrating that targeted noise can \emph{improve} a deep NIDS, and
\textbf{Experiment~B} (the analytical core, on the \emph{complete} dataset, over
20 random seeds, with both single- and multiple-feature corruption) mapping the
full space of effects that controlled corruption produces on a tabular and on a
time-series classifier.

\section{Background and Positioning}
% =====================================================================

The machine learning community has historically concentrated on the model
training stage, yet a growing body of evidence shows that data-quality issues
upstream of training often have a larger impact on final performance than the
choice of model. PuckTrick distinguishes itself from detection/cleaning tools
(DataAssert, CleanML, etc.) by its focus on the \emph{deliberate injection} of
orthogonal corruption categories, each parametrised by a target column, a
percentage, and a \emph{New}/\emph{Extended} variant for incremental
experiments. The empirical literature on each noise type is consistent: feature
noise causes a roughly monotone degradation proportional to the corrupted
feature's importance; label noise is widely regarded as more damaging than
feature noise; duplicates bias the model toward over-represented instances; and
outliers can destabilise training. Prior deep-learning work on CIC-IDS2018
(Random Forest, CNNs, LSTM/GRU, autoencoders) reports strong detection rates but
\emph{does not} systematically study how data-quality degradation affects
performance. This thesis fills that gap by introducing controlled PuckTrick
corruptions and measuring their impact on TabNet and a CNN-LSTM in a multi-task
configuration.

\section{Library Extension: Design, Challenges, and Performance}
% =====================================================================

The central design principle guiding the extension was \emph{functional
equivalence}: for every corruption method, the Spark implementation must accept
the same parametrisation and produce statistically equivalent output
distributions, so that Pandas and Spark results are directly comparable. The
migration had to abstract away Pandas idioms (row-level indexing, in-place
assignment, NumPy-backed sampling) and instead exploit Spark's defining traits:
\emph{lazy evaluation} (transformations are planned and optimised by the Catalyst
engine, executed only when an action is invoked), a distributed \emph{columnar}
DataFrame API that operates at the column rather than the row level, and
\emph{horizontal scalability} across cluster nodes.

\paragraph{Dual-backend architecture.}
The originally flat, Pandas-specific codebase was reorganised into a three-tier
modular layout (\texttt{pucktrick/core/}, \texttt{backends/pandas\_backend/},
\texttt{backends/spark\_backend/}). The single user-facing \texttt{PuckTrick}
class accepts a DataFrame and an engine selector (\texttt{Engine.AUTO},
\texttt{Engine.PANDAS}, \texttt{Engine.SPARK}); a \texttt{BackendFactory}
resolves the backend at runtime (in \texttt{AUTO} mode by PySpark availability
and estimated dataset size, with a $600$\,MB threshold). A static
\texttt{make\_remote\_cluster\_config} encapsulates all cluster configuration,
and a \texttt{BaseBackend} abstract class guarantees that future engines (Polars,
DuckDB) can be added without touching the public API.

\paragraph{The five corruption methods.}
Every method accepts a working copy of the DataFrame and a declarative
\emph{strategy dictionary} (the affected features, a row-selection criterion, the
percentage, the \texttt{new}/\texttt{extended} mode, and a perturbation
specification) and returns a tuple \texttt{(error\_code, modified\_df)}. The five
modules behave identically across backends: \texttt{duplicated} appends copies of
sampled rows (optionally producing \emph{near}-duplicates via text perturbations
such as word shuffling or abbreviation); \texttt{labels} flips binary targets or
replaces categorical targets with a different existing class; \texttt{missing}
nullifies the sampled cells; \texttt{noise} replaces values with plausible
in-domain alternatives (a different category for strings, a uniform draw in
$[\min,\max]$ for numerics, a random in-range date for datetimes); and
\texttt{outliers} injects type-dependent extreme values (beyond $\mu\pm4\sigma$
for numerics, the next integer beyond the maximum for categorical integers, a
sentinel string, or extreme timestamps).

\paragraph{Technical challenges.}
Several deep architectural differences between Pandas and Spark required
engineering solutions across the five modules: (i) the \emph{absence of
positional indices} in Spark was resolved by assigning each row a surrogate key
via \texttt{monotonically\_increasing\_id()} (\texttt{\_pucktrick\_row\_id});
(ii) \emph{lazy versus eager evaluation} forced a restructuring of the control
flow in \texttt{noisy} and \texttt{outliers}, where branching depends on column
properties that only an action can materialise; (iii) Spark's \emph{probabilistic
sampling} was made exact through an oversample-and-\texttt{limit(n)} strategy that
restores the precise requested percentage; (iv) divergent \emph{null semantics}
were handled with explicit casts and \texttt{isNull}/\texttt{isNotNull}
predicates; and (v) the \texttt{duplicated} method was rewritten to minimise the
number of Spark \emph{actions} (caching counts, replacing
\texttt{dropDuplicates().count()} with a single aggregation), eliminating the
redundant global shuffles that initially made it slower than Pandas. Functional
equivalence was certified by a suite of unit and cross-backend integration tests,
and validated on a real five-node Standalone Spark cluster (one master and four
workers; three workers with $4$ cores/$16$\,GB and one with $16$ cores/$32$\,GB),
configured through passwordless SSH, a \texttt{workers} file, per-worker resource
limits set in \texttt{spark-env.sh}, and the opening of ports $7077$ (cluster
communication) and $8080$ (Web UI).

\paragraph{Performance results.}
A benchmark on a synthetic $1{,}000{,}000$-row dataset with heterogeneous column
types (corruption rate $50\%$) revealed two clusters. For the
\emph{low-complexity} methods (\texttt{duplicated}, \texttt{missing}) Pandas is
faster ($\approx 0.5$\,s vs.\ $\approx 2$\,s for Spark), because Spark's fixed
DAG-compilation and serialization overhead ($1.2$--$1.5$\,s) cannot be amortised
on operations that fit in a single machine. For the \emph{high-complexity}
methods (\texttt{noise}, \texttt{outliers}, \texttt{labels}) the ranking reverses
dramatically: Spark is uniformly $2$--$8$\,s across all column types, whereas
Pandas is strongly type-dependent ($\text{float}<\text{int}<\text{string}<\text{date}$),
reaching $\approx 2{,}500$\,s on string/datetime columns. A break-even study
($1$M$\to$$64$M rows) confirmed that \texttt{duplicated} and \texttt{missing}
overtake Pandas only beyond $\approx 7$M rows, while the complex methods are
already faster below $1$M. A distributed stress test of \texttt{duplicated}
scaled near-linearly from $1$M to $128$M rows ($3.6$\,s $\to$ $84.6$\,s).
Crucially, running the \emph{whole} five-method pipeline sequentially, the Spark
backend is already $8.2\times$ faster than Pandas at one million rows, rising to
$29.1\times$ at $4$M, $40.9\times$ at $16$M and saturating at $40$--$43\times$
beyond $32$M (an Amdahl's-law plateau). This $\approx 8\times$ gain at the
one-million-row scale is precisely what made the experimental campaign of the two
empirical phases (almost $14{,}000$ trained models) computationally tractable,
binding the engineering contribution directly to the analytical one.

\section{Dataset Analysis}
% =====================================================================

After unification the dataset contains $16{,}173{,}208$ flow instances described
by $79$ purely statistical features (no application-layer payload) plus two
labels: \texttt{Label} (fine-grained attack type) and \texttt{label\_generic}
(binary benign/malicious). The class distribution is \emph{severely imbalanced}
(benign $\gg$ malign), which is why all evaluation is reported in terms of
imbalance-robust metrics rather than accuracy. Temporal analysis of the
\texttt{Timestamp} column shows that attacks are clustered into bursts (a
scripted, campaign-like structure), which validates treating the data as a time
series and motivates the CNN-LSTM. A completeness scan found only $\approx 0.37\%$
null/\texttt{inf} rows, removed by listwise deletion.

A feature-relevance pipeline then drove the two-branch design. A Pearson
correlation screen ($|r|\ge 0.85$) flagged \textbf{27} redundant features, and a
variance screen flagged \textbf{8} zero-variance features; both were removed for
\textbf{Experiment~A} (yielding a \textbf{44}-feature reduced set) but
\emph{retained} for \textbf{Experiment~B} (full \textbf{79} features), precisely
so that noise injection into uninformative features could be studied as a
control. By effective cardinality the full set comprises $11$ binary, $3$ categorical,
$64$ continuous and $1$ date feature ($7/2/34/1$ in the reduced set). A fragility
analysis based on skewness and kurtosis classified the vast majority of features
($\approx 57/79$) as \emph{high fragility}. A
feature--target analysis (point-biserial with the binary label, ANOVA F with the
multi-class label) revealed a central tension: the maximum binary correlation is
modest ($|\rho|\approx 0.43$, \texttt{Fwd Seg Size Min}), while the most
discriminative features for attack-type attribution are also the most fragile.
This \emph{fragility--relevance tension} is the hypothesis that the experiments
were designed to probe.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Models.}
TabNet was retained for its strong baseline F1 and its native, attention-based
global feature importance; the CNN-LSTM (adapted from Jablaoui et al.) for its
proven ability to learn window-level statistical signatures of attack campaigns.
Two further candidates, a pure LSTM and a Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT), were
tested and \emph{discarded}: the TFT in particular is structurally mismatched to
retrospective flow classification (it requires static and known-future
covariates absent here), and empirically collapsed its predictions to a single
class. The CNN-LSTM consumes 3D sliding windows ($T=50$, stride $S=10$), labelled
by their last flow; an important consequence is a $\approx 10{:}1$ asymmetry in
effective sample count (TabNet $\approx 269$k validation samples vs.\ CNN-LSTM
$\approx 27$k), which makes CNN-LSTM metrics intrinsically noisier.

\paragraph{HPO and feature selection.}
Hyperparameters were optimised with Optuna (TPE sampler and Median Pruner) on a
$0.1\%$ stratified sample ($\approx 16$k flows), maximising mean MCC (TabNet) or
mean F1 (CNN-LSTM) to counter the class imbalance ($9$ TabNet trials, $10$
CNN-LSTM trials). The best configurations (TabNet trial~7 for Experiment~B,
trial~1 for Experiment~A, reaching a mean MCC of $0.870$ on the validation
sample; CNN-LSTM trial~1) were persisted, with a linear batch-size scaling rule
(and a matching $\lambda_{\text{sparse}}$/learning-rate correction) applied when
moving to the larger training set.
Feature importance from both models was then combined: Experiment~A took the
\emph{intersection} of the two top-15 lists (a compact $8$-feature, high-signal
set), whereas Experiment~B used a \emph{combined average rank} with a
\emph{stratified} selection that deliberately spans importance tiers and feature
types, including zero-variance \emph{negative controls}, yielding a $9$-feature
working set plus \texttt{Timestamp}.

\paragraph{Splitting and baselines.}
A shuffle-free temporal split (sort by \texttt{Timestamp}, modular assignment
$g_i = i \bmod 3$) on a $10\%$ stratified sample ($\approx 1{,}614{,}745$ flows)
preserves chronological order \emph{and} all 15 attack classes, with
$66.7\%/16.7\%/16.7\%$ train/validation/test fractions; a \texttt{StandardScaler}
fitted only on the training partition prevents leakage. The clean baselines
(against which all noisy runs are compared) are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:baselines}.

\paragraph{Experimental grid.}
For every fully specified configuration (one feature or feature-group, one of the
five PuckTrick methods, one corruption percentage, one seed) \emph{both} models
are retrained from scratch and their metrics (MCC, F1, AUC, confusion matrices,
feature importance) persisted. Validation and test partitions remain clean
throughout: only the training partition is corrupted. Experiment~A uses the grid
$p_A\in\{1,5,10,20,30,50,75\}\%$ over $5$ seeds and $4$ corrupted columns
($525$ configurations $\Rightarrow$ $1{,}050$ models). Experiment~B uses
$p_B\in\{5,10,20,35,50,75\}\%$ over $20$ seeds, in a single-feature scenario
($10$ columns plus the two targets; $4{,}080$ configurations $\Rightarrow$
$8{,}160$ models) and a multiple-feature scenario ($6$ feature groups plus a
joint target group; $2{,}280$ configurations $\Rightarrow$ $4{,}560$ models).
The campaign totals \textbf{6{,}885 configurations} and \textbf{13{,}770 trained
models}. Each cell is summarised by its mean MCC with a Student-$t$ $95\%$
confidence half-width over its seeds, and tested for significance against the
clean baseline with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test ($p<0.05$); with only five
seeds Experiment~A cannot fall below the test's hard floor of $\approx 0.0625$,
so its large effects are read descriptively and \emph{confirmed} by the 20-seed
Experiment~B. The grid was run partly on a local GPU workstation and partly on an
HPC cluster; since each model skips training when its checkpoint already exists
(idempotent reruns) and out-of-memory crashes are a recurrent risk, a watchdog
process auto-restarted failed jobs from their last checkpoint.

\section{Experimental Results}
% =====================================================================

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experiment B: a behavioural map of noise effects}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Single-feature corruption.}
Three regimes emerge. \emph{(1) Broad resilience.} Corrupting any single input
feature leaves the binary MCC essentially unchanged across almost the entire
grid, especially for TabNet, whose sparse sequential attention simply reallocates
its mask toward the surviving clean features. The multi-class head pays a small,
systematic $-0.02$ to $-0.03$ penalty under almost any corruption (even of
zero-variance controls), confirming that fine-grained attack resolution is the
first capability to erode. The lone exception is \texttt{Dst Port} (the single
most discriminative column), whose corruption degrades both models and is flagged
highly significant ($p<10^{-4}$). \emph{(2) Beneficial corruption.} The most
striking result is that injecting \texttt{outliers} into \texttt{Timestamp}
\emph{improves} the CNN-LSTM monotonically and significantly ($\Delta\mathrm{MCC}$
up to $+0.147$ binary and $+0.236$ multi-class at high noise, $p<10^{-4}$). Since
\texttt{Timestamp} is dropped before the feature tensor is built, the corruption
does not pollute inputs; instead it permutes the rows \emph{before} the
chronological sort, weakening the within-window label auto-correlation that the
CNN-LSTM would otherwise exploit as a spurious \emph{temporal shortcut}. The
corruption thus acts as a targeted regulariser. The effect is absent for the
order-invariant TabNet, which has no such shortcut to remove. \emph{(3) Inert
duplication.} The row-level \texttt{duplicated} method has near-null effect, as
exact duplicates add no entropy and merely re-weight rows.

\paragraph{Label corruption.}
Corrupting the supervision signal is categorically different. As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:cliff}, flipping the \emph{binary} target is harmless up to
$\approx 35\%$ and then collapses off a cliff at $50\%$, descending into outright
\emph{inversion} at $75\%$ ($\Delta\mathrm{MCC}=-1.642$ for TabNet, a near-complete
sign reversal): exactly the phase transition a two-state symmetric flip predicts.
Corrupting the $15$-class \emph{multi-class} target, by contrast, only degrades
gradually, because a reassignment among many classes can never form a coherent
inverse mapping. The multi-task probe further exposes \emph{cross-head leakage}:
the binary collapse \emph{propagates} to the multi-class head, but only in the
binary$\rightarrow$multi-class direction and only for the CNN-LSTM (whose two
heads share a single context vector, $-0.536$ at $75\%$), while TabNet's
attention-disentangled heads barely move ($-0.050$). The operational lesson is
sharp: the integrity of the \emph{binary} label must be guaranteed above all.

\paragraph{Multiple-feature corruption.}
Compound corruption is additive-to-super-additive. On the worst group (six
important features) at $75\%$ \texttt{outliers}, the CNN-LSTM multi-class MCC
falls by $-0.526$ (the single most severe feature-level degradation in the
campaign), whereas TabNet loses only $\approx -0.05$ --- an order of magnitude
less --- because its per-step sparse attention re-routes around the corrupted
columns and demotes them in its importance ranking while \emph{promoting} clean
ones (notably the low-fragility \texttt{Fwd Seg Size Min}). Decisively, jointly
corrupting \emph{both} targets does \emph{not} reproduce the binary cliff: a
reassignment among attack classes preserves the benign/malicious distinction, so
the binary supervision stays largely intact. The impact of label noise therefore
depends not only on its quantity but on the precise \emph{mechanics} of how it is
realised.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experiment A: a real-world proof of concept}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

On the reduced, high-signal $8$-feature set, the regularisation phenomenon of
Experiment~B reappears across \emph{most} features rather than only
\texttt{Timestamp}. For the CNN-LSTM, the \texttt{outliers} method systematically
raises held-out MCC, with the largest and most consistent gains on the
multi-class task (e.g.\ $+0.353$ for \texttt{Timestamp}, and $\approx +0.17$ for
\texttt{FIN Flag Cnt} and \texttt{Down/Up Ratio}, with tight confidence margins).
TabNet, already near its ceiling, shows no systematic improvement, confirming
that the gain is specifically about \emph{regularising an overfit-prone temporal
model}.

The decisive contribution of Experiment~A, however, is the change of yardstick.
For a security system the proper measure is not the aggregate MCC but the
\emph{asymmetric operational cost} of errors: a false negative (a missed attack)
is far graver than a false positive (a false alarm). Read through this lens, a
\emph{model-independent} improvement emerges: corrupting the \texttt{Protocol}
column with \texttt{outliers} at $50\%$ consistently reduces the number of false
negatives for \emph{both} TabNet ($\approx 600$ fewer missed detections) and
CNN-LSTM ($\approx 200$), at the price of at most a negligible MCC loss. A
property of the dataset, identified through systematic analysis, can thus be
deliberately exploited to make a deep NIDS materially safer in deployment: the
proof of concept is validated.

\section{Conclusions}
% =====================================================================

All three objectives were met. \textbf{(1)} PuckTrick was successfully
re-engineered with an Apache Spark backend behind an unchanged public API, with
certified functional equivalence and a decisive, scalable speed-up
($8.2\times$ at one million rows, plateauing at $40$--$43\times$) that made the
$\approx 14$k-model campaign affordable. \textbf{(2)} Experiment~B produced a
coherent, statistically grounded behavioural map: robustness is an
\emph{architectural} property (TabNet's sparse attention makes it markedly more
robust than the CNN-LSTM); a controlled noise budget can be net-positive but only
within a narrow window (removing a learnable temporal shortcut); the two tasks
degrade at different rates (attack attribution is the more fragile); corrupting
the supervision signal is categorically more dangerous than corrupting inputs,
with a binary-label inversion cliff beyond $50\%$ and architecture-dependent
cross-head leakage; and the magnitude of degradation tracks feature importance so
faithfully that the protocol doubles as a model-agnostic importance probe.
\textbf{(3)} Experiment~A demonstrated a practical, model-independent real-world
gain (false-negative reduction via \texttt{outliers} on \texttt{Protocol}).

In closing, this thesis shows that controlled data corruption, far from being
merely a threat to be defended against, can be turned into a deliberate,
well-characterised \emph{tool}: one that probes what a model has learned, exposes
the boundaries of its robustness, and --- when wielded with knowledge of the data
and the cost structure of the task --- can make a deep-learning intrusion
detection system measurably safer.

% =====================================================================
\section{Future Work}
% =====================================================================

Several directions follow naturally. On the experimental side: extending the
false-negative/false-positive operational analysis of Experiment~A to the full
feature set and the multi-feature groups of Experiment~B, ideally validated in an
online or streaming detection setting; treating noise injection as a
\emph{tunable hyperparameter} searched directly for an operational objective
(e.g.\ minimal false negatives at bounded false-positive cost) rather than
discovered by exhaustive sweep; formally characterising and mitigating the
asymmetric multi-task contamination observed in the CNN-LSTM, motivating
architectural countermeasures such as attention-disentangled or gated
task-specific heads; and replicating the campaign on further architectures and
datasets to test how far the architecture-dependent conclusions generalise. On
the library-engineering side: adding new backends beneath the existing
\texttt{BackendFactory} (Polars or DuckDB for fast single-machine execution, a
GPU/RAPIDS \texttt{cuDF} backend for large in-memory workloads), and closing the
residual performance gaps --- lowering the $\approx 7$M-row break-even point of
the \texttt{duplicated} and \texttt{missing} methods, and porting the
date-column \texttt{noise} path to native Spark date handling.
